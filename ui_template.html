Open the model: how do pixels become boxes and names?

Training comes first. People provide examples with checked labels. A detector learns from animal locations and categories; a classifier learns from category labels. Training compares predictions with those labels and adjusts many numerical weights to reduce error. Useful patterns can include edges, textures, shapes, and combinations of features. A model can also learn misleading background clues.

Inference uses those learned weights. MegaDetector processes a resized version of the input and predicts candidate locations and scores. A box is four location numbers. Our particular compact model takes a 640 × 640 input, with padding to preserve the photo's shape. Its end-to-end architecture handles selection internally; other detectors often add a step called non-maximum suppression to reduce overlapping candidates. Duplicate boxes can still occur.

Species identification is a separate task. Here we use SpeciesNet's classifier on MegaDetector crops. The full SpeciesNet software can also run its own detection and geographic processing; this lab deliberately shows the crop-classification step separately. Classifier scores and detector scores mean different things and should not be compared as if they were the same measurement.

AI is still code. People wrote the pixel-editing instructions, model architecture, training process, and interface. The difference is that many pattern-recognition rules are represented by weights learned from examples, rather than a person writing an exact rule for every ear or tail. This activity uses pretrained models; moving a slider does not train them.

Does SpeciesNet need a species list from me?

The pretrained model already has its list. Our bundled classifier ranks 2,498 learned labels, including species, broader animal groups, and non-animal labels such as blank. You do not supply deer, raccoon, and coyote as choices. The model can only return labels in its trained vocabulary; it cannot learn a new species because someone types its name.

A custom model has a custom list. If researchers fine-tune a classifier on their own labeled species, that model predicts from the classes it was trained to distinguish. An animal outside that list may be assigned a wrong known label. A local allowlist or geographic filter can change how existing outputs are reported; it does not teach new visual patterns. Google's optional target-label scores also refer to labels the model already knows.

This lab applies no species shortlist or geographic filter. A human source label is kept separate from the model input and prediction. Google's model explanation · Classifier and optional target-label scores

How would we train SpeciesNet for our own species?

Fine-tuning continues learning from checked photographs. A species-name list alone is not training data. This lab runs pretrained models; it does not train a new classifier.

  1. Choose the classes. Decide which species or broader groups the project needs and what should require human review.
  2. Collect and label examples. Use many independent encounters, camera sites, seasons, poses, and day/night conditions. Check species labels and the actual animal crops; avoid giving every crop the same label in a mixed-species photo.
  3. Keep an honest test. Separate training, validation, and final test data by encounter and camera site before tuning. Repeated frames of one animal are not independent evidence.
  4. Fine-tune a classifier. Use SpeciesNet's learned image features as a starting point, train an output layer for the chosen classes, and optionally adjust more layers. An optimizer changes weights using errors on the labeled training crops.
  5. Check mistakes. Use validation data to choose training settings; then measure per-class precision, recall, and confusions once on the held-out test. Include unfamiliar animals and false detector crops in review testing.
  6. Keep model and labels together. Save the checkpoint, class order, preprocessing, and evaluation. A custom checkpoint needs its matching inference code; changing a text label file does not change the trained model.

Google links a DIY SpeciesNet fine-tuning tutorial. It is a separate training project, with its own setup and data preparation. Our small teaching photo collection is for demonstration, not a sufficient training set or field benchmark.

Three things every wildlife detective should know

Open a field note to investigate further.
01 · A box is a prediction

A score is a model score, not a probability that the answer is right. No box means “not detected at this cutoff,” not “there is no animal.” Lower the cutoff: you may recover a missed animal, but you may also show a false alarm. One photo cannot tell us how accurate the detector is overall.

Darkness and blur do not always lower scores smoothly. Models can be surprisingly sturdy—or surprisingly wrong.

02 · The camera changes the clues

Color, body outline, texture, and the number of pixels covering the animal can all matter. A large animal far away may occupy fewer pixels than a small animal nearby. A photo alone does not measure body length without a scale or distance information.

These are controlled image edits, not real camera tests. More megapixels cannot fix every problem: focus, motion, light, distance, and a clear view matter too. Upscaling makes bigger pixels; it does not recover missing detail.

03 · New habitats need new evidence

Grass, branches, snow, glare, and camouflage can hide useful clues. Training can include checked examples of partly hidden animals and many habitats; testing must use new photos and camera sites. A completely hidden animal may leave no visible evidence.

Black-and-white is not infrared. Near-infrared night cameras record reflected light. Thermal cameras sense emitted infrared radiation and need different sensors and suitable models. This lab does not simulate either.

Underwater is a different camera problem. Waterproof, depth-rated housings, lighting, water clarity, scattering, and color loss matter. There is no universal depth where a camera stops seeing. Underwater animal detection needs relevant training and testing; our land-animal examples do not demonstrate it.

A useful knob, not a magic number

Why adjust the cutoff—and which setting is best?

The cutoff is an acceptance rule

The detector assigns a score to each candidate box. A cutoff, or score threshold, means “keep candidates whose scores are at least this value.” It filters predictions; it does not crop the photograph or change the model's learned weights.

A lower minimum may rescue faint detections, but can also admit false alarms. A higher minimum can reduce false alarms, but discard real animals. Neither move guarantees a better answer. 0.30 is this lab's teaching default, not a proven best setting.

PrecisionOf the animal boxes we kept, how many were correct?
RecallOf the animals that were really present, how many did we find?

Choose it using checked examples

First choose the goal. A conservation team might accept more false alarms to miss fewer rare animals, then ask volunteers to review them. Another task may have stricter limits on review time.

Use a separate, representative validation collection where people marked real animal boxes—including empty scenes, difficult cases, seasons, and camera sites. Try multiple cutoffs and measure both missed animals and false alarms. Choose a tradeoff for that goal, then check the selected setting once on an untouched test collection.

There is no universal optimal cutoff. It depends on the model, images, species, and costs of mistakes. Do not pick a field-research threshold from this small selected demo.

For older students: how to compare candidate boxes fairly
  1. Have people label animal locations. Keep photographs from the same encounter together, and hold out camera sites when testing new places.
  2. At each proposed threshold, match predictions to real boxes using a stated overlap criterion, such as intersection over union (IoU). IoU compares the overlapping area with the total area covered by both boxes. Use one-to-one matching so two boxes around one animal do not count as two correct animals. This overlap rule is separate from the model-score cutoff.
  3. Count correct detections (true positives), unmatched predictions (false positives), and missed animals (false negatives). Plot precision versus recall or tabulate the tradeoff.
  4. Choose an objective before comparing settings: for example, high recall within a review budget; a weighted cost for missed animals and false alarms; or F1, which balances precision and recall. Report the criterion and its limits.
  5. Freeze the threshold, evaluate the untouched test set, and keep difficult or uncertain results for human review. If the camera type or habitat changes, validate again. Detector and classifier acceptance rules may need separate evaluation.

The team's questions, answered

Ask the wildlife AI lab

Open any question. These answers explain what this experiment demonstrates—and what needs other equipment or evidence.

What actually is AI? How is it different from ordinary code?

AI is software designed for tasks such as recognizing patterns, predicting, planning, or generating material. Machine learning is one way to build AI: training adjusts numerical parameters from examples. Deep learning uses layered neural networks; computer vision analyzes images. A chatbot generates text, while these models recognize image patterns.

Conventional code can explicitly multiply every pixel by 0.10 to darken a picture. The detector uses learned weights to suggest boxes instead of a programmer writing a complete list of animal shapes. Both still run as computer programs; some AI systems also use human-written rules.

What can AI do—and what can it not do?

It can help find likely animals in large photo collections and suggest labels for review. It can miss an animal, invent a false alarm, draw a poor box, or give an unfamiliar animal a confident wrong name. It cannot reliably recover evidence that was never recorded or see through an opaque obstruction.

Finding an animal is not identifying its species; identifying its species is not recognizing a unique individual. More boxes or repeated photographs do not automatically mean more animals live in a place. People check predictions and design the conservation study.

What makes animals easy or hard to spot: color, size, or shape?

All can matter, together with texture, pose, lighting, and background. Ears, tails, outlines, and patterns can help, but hidden parts or unfamiliar views can remove clues. Models can also learn misleading background shortcuts.

Size in the picture means pixels, not actual body length. A distant deer can cover fewer pixels than a nearby squirrel. Physical measurements require scale, distance, or camera calibration. Removing RGB color in this lab tests one clue; it does not make an infrared image.

Which habitats are harder for AI?

Tall grass, branches, camouflage, clutter, glare, snow, rain, and fog can hide or alter clues. An open view may make an animal more visible, but no habitat is automatically easy for every model.

A model trained on familiar forest trails can struggle at a new camera site or in a different environment. Test using checked local images and camera sites not used for training. A model finding fewer boxes in thick vegetation does not prove fewer animals live there.

What cameras work well? How much resolution or clarity is needed?

Choose a camera for the research question, subject size, distance, lighting, and environment. Good focus, exposure, suitable optics, camera placement, and enough pixels across the animal matter. Motion blur can erase clues even in a high-megapixel photograph.

There is no single megapixel requirement that guarantees detection. This MegaDetector variant resizes inputs to 640 × 640 with padding, so extra source resolution is not all used directly. A small, clear animal and a large, blurry one present different problems. Downsampling loses detail; enlarging afterward does not restore it.

Does AI work with heat or infrared images too?

It can, with suitable sensors and models tested on the actual imagery. Near-infrared night cameras commonly illuminate a scene with invisible infrared light and record its reflections. Thermal cameras detect emitted infrared energy associated with surface temperatures; that is a different measurement.

A camera trap's passive infrared trigger decides when to take a photograph. It is different from the vision software that examines the resulting pixels. Grayscale and rainbow filters do not create infrared or temperature data. This lab does not establish thermal or near-infrared performance.

Can AI be trained to find camouflaged animals?

Yes, training or fine-tuning can include real, checked examples of camouflaged and partly hidden animals, with bounding-box labels, varied poses, seasons, backgrounds, and empty scenes. Then evaluate on separate encounters and camera sites.

Representative examples can help, but do not guarantee detection. If camouflage or an opaque branch removes all usable clues, the model has no magical way to see them. The gray strip in this lab demonstrates obstruction; it is not a faithful simulation of natural camouflage.

Can cameras see underwater—and how deep?

Yes, suitable cameras in waterproof, depth-rated housings can photograph underwater animals. There is no universal maximum depth for visibility: light, water clarity, suspended particles, camera-to-animal distance, and illumination matter. The housing also has its own pressure/depth rating.

Water absorbs and scatters light, changes colors and contrast, and bends light. Looking from above a reflective surface differs from placing a camera near an animal underwater. Underwater AI needs appropriate training and testing on real underwater images; a land camera-trap model should not be assumed reliable there. A blue tint is not an underwater experiment.

How does this help wildlife—and which research is actually local?

The Illinois presentation discusses Blanding's turtles in DuPage and Lake County, piping plovers at Montrose Beach, Cook County coyotes, and river otters in Cook and Will County. These local research questions can require different tools, including field observations, radio/GPS tracking, and cameras.

That division of work is transferable. Its species list and research findings are not Illinois findings.
