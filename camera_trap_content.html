Meet the camera behind the photographs

A camera trap can keep watch while people are away.

A weather-resistant camera is fastened to a tree and aimed across a wildlife path. In a common design, a passive infrared sensor provides the trigger, and a separate lens and image sensor record the picture.

The camera is waiting. Move the animal into view to try the trigger.

Illustration only. The shaded area is a simplified sensing area, not a beam emitted by the PIR sensor.

  1. 1 · Sense a change

    A moving animal with a different surface temperature from the background changes the infrared radiation reaching the passive infrared (PIR) sensor. The sensor can trigger the camera. It does not identify the species.

  2. 2 · Use daylight

    In daylight, the camera uses available visible light to record a usually color photograph. The motion trigger can work in daylight or darkness.

  3. 3 · Save the photograph

    The image sensor records the scene and the camera saves the file, often to a memory card. Later, people or AI can inspect the photograph.

A saved camera-trap example

This illustrates a common PIR camera with infrared LEDs. Other designs use white flash, an active beam, or timed photography. A weak temperature contrast, distance, speed, or camera settings can prevent a trigger. An infrared-lit night photograph records reflected light; a thermal camera instead maps emitted heat radiation.
