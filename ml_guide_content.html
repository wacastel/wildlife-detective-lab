Build carefully · test honestly · keep checking

How to make machine learning useful

A model is one part of a working system. Success depends on the question, photographs, checked answers, evaluation, and people using the result. A more complicated network cannot repair missing evidence or an unfair test.

  1. 1

    Define success before training

    Ask a specific question: find animal visits, identify a species, or locate an object? Compare with a simple rule or manual review. Decide which mistakes matter more: extra photographs to review or missed animals.

  2. 2

    Collect the evidence you will need

    Use permitted, representative images with checked labels. Include empty frames, rare species, day/night views, difficult poses, and expected cameras. Correct wrong labels; more copies of one easy encounter do not create diverse evidence.

  3. 3

    Split encounters before learning

    Keep neighboring frames, duplicates, and crops from one visit together. Hold out camera sites when testing a new location. Train on training data, choose settings on validation data, and use an untouched test set for the final check. Why separate the sets?

  4. 4

    Change one thing, then measure

    Watch training and validation loss, inspect mistakes by species and condition, and record each experiment. Choose a checkpoint and cutoff using validation evidence. Repeatedly tuning against the test set makes it another validation set.

  5. 5

    Keep the whole recipe consistent

    Save the model version, class order, resize/padding, color channels, scaling, and thresholds. Preserve exact input and crop hashes. Different preprocessing or swapped class names can spoil otherwise good weights.

  6. 6

    Review, monitor, and retest

    Check uncertain results and a sample of apparently empty frames. Log corrections and watch new sites, seasons, and cameras for changes. Retest after changing the model or pipeline; a past score does not guarantee future performance.

Common gotchas: a symptom, a reason, a better check

“The test score is amazing, but a new camera fails.”

Possible reason: related frames leaked across splits, or the model learned backgrounds. Better check: separate encounters and hold out sites. Camera-trap research documents this new-location problem.

“Training improves while validation gets worse.”

Possible reason: overfitting. Better check: inspect both curves, simplify or regularize the model, improve representative data, and choose a checkpoint using validation evidence. More steps are not automatically better.

“The crop classifier confidently names the wrong species.”

Possible reason: a poor detector box, a missing species, or a misleading crop. Better check: inspect the original, box, and exact crop. Check each pipeline stage; multiplying two model scores does not establish a correct-identification probability.

“It works on clear photos, then misses night visitors.”

Possible reason: different lighting, blur, tiny subjects, or new camera imagery. Better check: evaluate real examples from those conditions. Artificial blur and grayscale teach useful questions but cannot establish infrared performance.

What machine learning cannot promise

A confident answer can be wrong.

A score is not proof. Check calibration before treating scores as reliable probabilities. The classroom CNN must choose among three labels; an unfamiliar fox can still receive a confident deer, raccoon, or coyote guess.

Missing information stays missing.

A model cannot reliably reconstruct hidden evidence. Boxes do not count unique individuals, establish population size, explain a cause, or demonstrate thermal/underwater ability. Each new task needs appropriate measurements and evaluation.

People and context remain essential.

A person box does not identify someone or establish their intentions. Respect image permissions and privacy. Conservation conclusions also need survey effort, camera placement, and ecological expertise.

When should you choose another approach?

  • A simpler method meets the need: a small collection may be faster to review manually; predictable rules may solve a well-defined task.
  • You cannot assemble suitable evidence: pause if permission, representative labels, or an independent evaluation are missing.
  • Mistakes cannot be safely checked: do not hand consequential decisions to an unvalidated model with no review or fallback.
  • The task asks for information the system cannot provide: use better sensors, field observations, or a different research design.

Useful automation has a way to say “uncertain,” ask a person, and recover from mistakes. Google's Rules of ML recommends beginning with clear metrics and simple approaches.
