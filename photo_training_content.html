Interactive training · real photos, a small classroom model

Watch a photograph become a prediction—and a learning signal.

This lesson really updates a tiny classifier in your browser. It uses a 4 × 4 grid of average red, green, and blue values to learn three teaching labels: deer, raccoon, and coyote. Its 144 weights and three biases start at zero. MegaDetector and SpeciesNet keep their existing weights.

Preparing the real photo examples…

Each update uses the same six labeled training photos. Choosing another photo, cell, or color channel changes the view. Train buttons update the learned weights; Reset returns them to zero. The held-out photos never supply gradients.

See the exact training calculation

Split the full photograph into a 4 × 4 grid. In each cell, average the red, green, and blue pixel values, then calculate x = average / 255 − 0.5 for each channel. That produces 48 input features in the range −0.5 to +0.5. These hand-designed color features remain fixed during training.

For each of three output labels, calculate z[k] = Σ(w[k,j] × x[j]) + b[k], then p[k] = exp(z[k]) / Σ exp(z). The checked label is a one-hot target: 1 for its class, 0 for the other classes. The photo's cross-entropy loss is −log(p[checked label]).

For each training photo, the score gradient is p[k] − y[k]. Average the six photos' gradients to obtain dw[k,j] = mean((p[k] − y[k]) × x[j]) and db[k] = mean(p[k] − y[k]). A step applies w -= learning_rate × dw and b -= learning_rate × db. The model updates 144 weights and three biases: 147 adjustable parameters.

The weights change together using the six-photo mean loss, so one selected photo's loss need not improve at every step. A large learning rate can overshoot. Repeating updates can fit these few photos closely while still making mistakes on unfamiliar images. This classroom calculation trains a small image classifier; animal-box detection and the larger wildlife networks use much richer architectures and training collections.
